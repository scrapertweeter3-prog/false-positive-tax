A detector that flags 8 percent of honest users is priced like a tax

AI-text detectors get sold on their catch rate, but the number that decides whether one survives contact with real traffic is the false-positive rate. At 2 percent, a moderation queue handed 5,000 submissions a day buries 100 real writers daily under manual review, and the pastagi.com detector work treats that as a serving cost: the queue hours, the appeals, the trust burned with every wrongly flagged author.

Run the same screen against a classroom of 200 essays and the arithmetic gets worse because the base rate of machine text is low. Assume the detector catches 90 percent of the 20 AI-written submissions and misfires on 8 percent of the 180 honest ones. It raises 14.4 false alarms against 18 true ones, so 44 percent of the flagged pile did nothing wrong, and one appeal letter per flagged student is the minimum cost of keeping the tool.

The fix is sequencing rather than a better threshold. Detectors earn their keep as a cheap first pass that routes the top few percent of scores into a human read, never as the verdict itself. A 0.9-and-above-only lane trims the false-positive exposure sharply while keeping most of the catch, because the confidence band is where the honest writers cluster.

Before any detector ships, someone should publish the confusion matrix on local writing, not the vendor demo set. Two numbers settle most arguments: the share of flagged items that were genuinely machine-written, and the share of honest authors who got flagged anyway. If either number would embarrass the buyer in a parent meeting or a court filing, the tool is not ready, whatever the marketing page claims.
